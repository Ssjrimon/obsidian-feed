# 2026 Annual Threat Assessment — key points

Source: Office of the Director of National Intelligence, unclassified Annual Threat Assessment of the U.S. Intelligence Community, March 2026. Information as of 14 March 2026. This is a short brief of the official report, not the full document.

Official PDF: https://archive.dni.gov/files/ODNI/documents/assessments/ATA-2026-Unclassified-Report.pdf

## What matters most

- The IC's top Homeland concerns for the coming year are transnational crime and illicit drugs, migration, Islamist terrorism, major-power competition, and WMD.
- Mexico-based cartels remain the main suppliers of fentanyl, methamphetamine, heroin, and cocaine to the U.S. Synthetic opioid deaths fell nearly 30 percent in the year to September 2025 (still more than 38,000 deaths). Southwest-border fentanyl seizures by weight are down 56 percent since early 2025.
- Southwest-border migrant encounters fell 79 percent in 2025 versus 2024, and January 2026 encounters were down 83.8 percent from January 2025. Underlying drivers of migration are largely unchanged.
- The most likely Homeland terrorist attack is a U.S.-based lone offender inspired by foreign extremist propaganda, not a trained overseas operative. Al-Qa‘ida and ISIS are weaker than at their peaks but still intend to target the U.S.
- China, Russia, and North Korea are fielding more missiles that can reach the Homeland. The IC projects threats to the Homeland rising from more than 3,000 missiles now to more than 16,000 by 2035.
- China is the most capable AI competitor and aims to lead globally by 2030. A cryptographically relevant quantum computer, which no country has built yet, could break today's encryption.
- China is the most active cyber threat to U.S. government, private, and critical-infrastructure networks. Russia is a persistent advanced cyber and intelligence threat. Iran, North Korea, and ransomware groups also pose serious risk.
- China, Russia, Iran, and North Korea each treat the U.S. as a strategic competitor, but the IC says "adversary alignment" overstates how deep their cooperation is.

## Homeland and security

- Sinaloa and CJNG dominate U.S.-bound drug supply. Ports of entry are still the main crossing points. Colombia remains the world's largest cocaine producer. China and India remain the main sources of fentanyl precursors and pill-press equipment, despite new Chinese export controls after the October 2025 Busan meeting and increased Indian cooperation.
- Tren de Aragua expanded with U.S.-bound migration and has targeted Venezuelan communities in the U.S. MS-13 remains established in U.S. cells. Haitian gangs have fired on the U.S. Embassy in Port-au-Prince.
- Cuba and Haiti could produce new migration surges if conditions worsen. Human smugglers are adapting as enforcement tightens.
- Al-Qa‘ida is estimated at 15,000–28,000 members worldwide; ISIS at 12,000–18,000, with most recent growth in Africa. AQAP, ISIS-Khorasan, and ISIS in Syria are the affiliates most likely to support external plots.
- Iran and Iranian-aligned groups, including HAMAS and Lebanese Hizballah, have been badly degraded but can still strike U.S. and allied interests in the Middle East. The Huthis and Iraqi Shia militias remain a regional threat.
- Russia is the primary Arctic challenge: it holds about half the Arctic coastline, hosts much of its second-strike nuclear force on the Kola Peninsula, and has the world's largest icebreaker fleet. China is expanding a more limited Arctic presence, often via Russia.

## Tech and cyber

- AI is already used for targeting and decision-making in recent conflicts. Advanced chips are concentrated in a few regions, so domestic design and production are a geopolitical priority.
- Early quantum computers would offer a major intelligence and encryption-breaking edge. The U.S., China, the EU, Japan, and the UK are investing heavily. Quantum-resistant encryption is a priority before a cryptographically relevant machine exists.
- China has overtaken Russia as the key space competitor. Competitors are building counterspace weapons; jamming and cyber attacks on satellites are becoming more common. Russia is developing a nuclear anti-satellite capability, which would damage all countries' satellites if detonated in space.
- North Korean cyber operations steal funds (at least $1 billion a year, including crypto heists) for weapons programs and use IT workers with falsified credentials. Ransomware groups are shifting to faster, high-volume attacks.

## Weapons and regions

- Russia has the largest and most diverse nuclear stockpile and is modernizing it. China is expanding and diversifying its nuclear force. North Korea is expanding its arsenal and has tested ICBMs that can reach the entire U.S. Homeland. India is developing longer-range nuclear delivery systems. Pakistan is improving missiles that could eventually reach beyond South Asia.
- Before Operation Epic Fury, Iran was advancing missiles, was non-compliant with chemical-weapons and IAEA nuclear obligations, and had not dropped offensive biological R&D. The IAEA confirmed damage to entrance buildings at Natanz and did not expect a radiological release. The IC is still assessing Iran's remaining WMD capabilities.
- Russia has used chemical weapons in Ukraine, suspended New START data exchanges (while still holding to central numeric limits), deratified the Comprehensive Nuclear Test Ban Treaty, and declared nuclear weapons in Belarus.
- China-Taiwan: the IC assesses Chinese leaders do not currently plan an invasion in 2027 and have no fixed unification timeline, though Beijing still ties unification to "national rejuvenation" by 2049 and prefers to achieve it without force if possible. A conflict would disrupt semiconductor supply and global markets.
- China is tightening control in the South China Sea, including at Scarborough Reef. Tensions with Japan rose after Prime Minister Takaichi called a Taiwan invasion a "survival-threatening situation" for Japan.
- Russia has the upper hand in Ukraine and sees little reason to stop while it is gaining ground. Continuation raises the risk of a Russia-NATO spiral, including sabotage (the IC cites a railway explosion in Poland in November 2025) and nuclear signaling. North Korean troops have gained combat experience in the war.
- In the Middle East, HAMAS is degraded but has used the cease-fire to restore some capability in Gaza. Hizballah is under Israeli pressure and facing uncertain Iranian support. The Huthis remain able to threaten Red Sea traffic. ISIS and al-Qa‘ida will try to exploit gaps in Syria.
- In Africa, al-Shabaab has pressed closer to Mogadishu. Al-Qa‘ida elements in the Sahel have blockaded Bamako. ISIS affiliates in West Africa and the Sahel have intensified attacks and moved closer to cities with a U.S. presence.
