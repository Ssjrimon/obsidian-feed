# 2025 National Security Strategy — key points

Source: The White House, National Security Strategy of the United States of America, November 2025. This is a short brief of the official strategy, not the full document.

Official PDF: https://www.whitehouse.gov/wp-content/uploads/2025/12/2025-National-Security-Strategy.pdf

## What the United States wants

- Survive as a sovereign republic, with full control of borders and immigration, and protection from military attack and hostile foreign influence, including espionage, predatory trade, trafficking, and influence operations.
- Field the world's most capable military and a modern nuclear deterrent, plus next-generation missile defenses including Golden Dome for the Homeland.
- Rebuild the industrial base, dominate energy production, stay first in science and technology (especially AI, biotech, and quantum), and protect intellectual property.
- Core foreign-policy interests: a Western Hemisphere free of hostile incursion (a "Trump Corollary" to the Monroe Doctrine); a free and open Indo-Pacific with secure supply chains; a Europe able to provide for its own security; no adversarial power dominating Middle East energy or chokepoints, without "forever wars"; and U.S. technology standards leading globally.

## Principles

- America First: a focused national interest, not an attempt to manage every global issue.
- Peace through strength, with a high bar for military intervention.
- Flexible realism: work with states whose systems differ, without imposing social change.
- Primacy of nation-states and U.S. sovereignty over intrusive transnational institutions.
- Prevent any power from becoming dominant enough to threaten U.S. interests, without seeking U.S. global domination.
- Policy that is pro-American worker, insists on fair treatment from allies (including much higher ally defense spending), and promotes competence and merit.

## Priorities

- End mass migration; treat border security as a primary element of national security, including terrorism, drugs, espionage, and trafficking.
- Protect core rights at home, especially speech, religion, and self-government, and press like-minded countries to do the same.
- Shift burdens to wealthy allies. NATO's Hague Commitment is 5 percent of GDP on defense.
- Use presidential diplomacy to settle wars and realign partners, including conflicts the strategy lists as addressed in 2025.
- Economic security: balanced and reciprocal trade; secure critical supply chains and minerals; reindustrialization and reshoring; a revived defense industrial base that can produce both cheap mass systems and high-end weapons; energy dominance in oil, gas, coal, and nuclear; and continued U.S. financial-market leadership.

## Regions

- Western Hemisphere: reassert the Monroe Doctrine, keep non-hemispheric competitors from holding forces or strategic assets, and "enlist and expand" regional partners to control migration, stop cartels, and near-shore manufacturing.
- Asia: compete economically with China, keep the Indo-Pacific free and open, and avoid military confrontation while building alliances. The strategy treats the earlier bet that trade would liberalize China as a failure.
- Europe: wants a more capable, sovereign Europe that spends on its own defense, manages relations with Russia, and reverses what the strategy calls civilizational decline (low growth, migration strain, and loss of confidence).
- Middle East: shift burdens and build peace. The strategy says energy and superpower-competition reasons for U.S. primacy there have faded, Iran has been weakened, and the U.S. should not remain the region's first responder.
- Africa: move from aid to trade and investment, especially energy and critical minerals; help settle selected conflicts; stay alert to Islamist terrorism without long-term U.S. deployments.
